## Reproducibility, and the Half I Keep Deferring

*Week of August 17, 2026 · DREAM fellowship, Chilton lab*

Two things forced clarity this week, one accidental and one overdue.

The accidental one: my laptop was water-damaged and about to be wiped, which turned an abstract question — *is this a standalone project?* — into an urgent one. Could someone recreate the whole system from the code alone? Auditing for that was clarifying. The boards render on the Python standard library with no dependencies, which is the reproducible core. The learning pipeline imports a helper module that isn't in the repo, so those scripts don't run standalone, and I documented that honestly rather than papering over it. Then I made it a real repository with a README, a design-notes file that captures *why* each choice was made, and a `.gitignore` so the scratch renders and screenshots don't pollute the history. The project now lives on GitHub as `scheduling-agent`. Forcing a system to survive the loss of the machine it was built on is a good test of whether the ideas actually live in the artifact or just in your head.

I also fixed a bug that turned out to be conceptual, not cosmetic. Mid-series — replies still trickling in — the board was firing its "relax the constraint, go small-ball" escalation while people simply hadn't answered yet. It was conflating *waiting* with *on fire*. Those are different states: if there's still a live candidate for the open position, you chase; only when there's genuinely no one left to ask do you relax the mix. Encoding that distinction is a small change that maps onto a real coordinator's judgment — don't concede the shape of the team just because the room is quiet.

But the honest headline this week is a scorecard on myself. Go back to the four reasons I gave for coordination being the right testbed. Three of them — private preferences, unreliable commitment, no infeasible state — the system now expresses well. The fourth, **endogenous participation**, I've barely touched, and it's the one I called "the interesting one." The idea that *your allocation decisions change who's willing to participate next time* is still not in the model. The mechanism that would capture it is sitting in my pending pile with a clear name: tier secrecy — don't tell people they're the fallback — and, more importantly, modeling that inviting someone as a role player *raises their probability of flaking*. That's the loop from the most surprising finding in the earlier post, the simulated players who got passed over and started holding out. I have built the optimization and the interface for it carefully. I have not built the part where the decision changes the decision environment.

**What I learned.** Reproducibility is a forcing function: the discipline of "could a stranger rebuild this?" surfaces every place where the real knowledge is still trapped in your head or your session history rather than in the repo. And self-audit against your own stated thesis is uncomfortable and necessary — I can render a constrained optimization board with real taste, and I've spent five weeks perfecting the half of the problem that's a solved optimization while deferring the half that's the actual research question. That's next.
