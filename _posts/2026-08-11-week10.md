## Constraints Have a Shape

*Week of August 11, 2026 · DREAM fellowship, Chilton lab*

This week broke an assumption baked into everything before it: that "best N" is a meaningful target. It isn't, once the roster has *composition*.

A real team isn't the top five by value. It's two guards, two forwards, a center. So the cut stopped being top-N and became a **per-position assignment**. The consequence is subtle and, I think, the most important idea of the summer so far: value becomes *marginal-given-need*. A high-rated player who plays a position you've already filled loses his slot to a lower-rated player who fills an empty one. "Best" is only defined relative to the shape you're trying to field. The single most persuasive case in the demo is the center: the number says Sam (84, but flaky), the read says Priya (80, but reliable) — and because it's the only center slot, that one call decides whether the constraint is even satisfiable.

I made the constraint two-tiered. Positions are hard by default. Only when a required position genuinely can't be filled does the mix become infeasible and escalate to soft — "go small-ball?" — which is the same needs-your-call pattern as everything else. You don't relax a constraint silently; you flag that it's about to break and let the organizer decide.

The other half of the week was a layout fight in service of one principle: **MECE, and let hierarchy do the work.** I split the board into a left column that is pure decision — the verdict, the constraints, the assignment, the one contested call — and a middle column that is the full ranked rolodex of people, where per-person detail lives *once*. Met constraints recede to a quiet line; the open or contested slot expands. An early version rendered every position as a chunky "2G / 2F" box, which read as noise; the fix was to spell out "2 Guards ✓" and reserve a box only for a slot that's actually open. Good visual hierarchy is mostly the discipline of making the settled things get out of the way.

**What I learned.** Constraints aren't a headcount; they're a shape, and optimization against a shape behaves differently — the marginal value of a candidate depends on what you've already got, not just on how good they are. And the hardest part of showing this wasn't the assignment logic, it was the restraint: saying each thing exactly once, letting the resolved constraints fade, spending the visual budget only where the decision actually is.
