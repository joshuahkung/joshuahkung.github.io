## The Hard Region

*Week of July 21, 2026 · DREAM fellowship, Chilton lab*

Last week's post laid out the goal: an agent that learns not just what you'd decide but *why*, and earns the right to act incrementally. This week I turned that lens on my own demo — and found it was quietly cheating.

The organizer view I'd built showed a coordinator whether they were "ready": did we have enough people, a ball, a time. It looked useful. But the scenario I was showing it on was four friends the organizer knew well, picking three. That decision has almost no entropy. When one option dominates, or when you already know everyone, there's nothing to decide, and a decision-support tool is dead weight. I had built a tool and demonstrated it on precisely the class of problem where the tool is unnecessary.

The reframing that fixed this: **a decision UI only earns its value in the hard region of the decision.** Constraint satisfaction — do we have N, a ball, a time — is the easy half. It's a binary check. The hard half is optimization: *is this the best feasible set, and what am I trading off at the margin?* That's the half the interface has to earn, and it's the half I'd skipped.

The difficulty lives in one place: the **cut line**, the boundary between in and out. The obvious starters need no UI. The contested slot needs all of it. And the canonical hard case is deceptively small — 80 versus 81. A single number can't separate two candidates that close; the gap is inside the noise. To decide, you have to pull in orthogonal dimensions the scalar was hiding: reliability, flake history, fit, the morale cost of benching someone. So I built the cell to stay calm when the answer is obvious and expand — surfacing the tie-break dimensions for just the contested two or three — only when it's genuinely hard. Difficulty drives information density.

**What I learned.** Designing the *demo* is part of designing the *system*. An instance is only hard when three things hold at once: enough candidates that the organizer can't hold them in their head, unfamiliarity so the learned signals are the only tie-breakers, and a real cut fight where the number and the reliability disagree. I'd been testing on an instance with none of those. I replaced it with twelve candidates for six slots, an organizer who doesn't know them, and a near-tie at the boundary. Only then does the apparatus I've been building — the agent that *learns the people* — have anything to do. If you design for "I know everyone and have four options," you've designed for the case that proves nothing.
